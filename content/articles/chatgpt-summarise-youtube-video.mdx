<AnswerBox>
Use the transcript rather than the video. YouTube provides one for most videos: open the description
and click Show transcript, then copy it. Paste that into ChatGPT and you can summarise it, pull the
main claims out, or find the part you actually needed, with timestamps.
</AnswerBox>

## Getting the transcript

<Steps>
  <Step title="Open the video description">
    Click the description area below the video to expand it.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Find Show transcript">
    Usually near the bottom of the expanded description. Most videos with captions have one.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Copy it">
    The panel opens beside the video. Select the text and copy. There is a toggle to hide timestamps
    if you prefer, though keeping them is usually more useful.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Paste it into ChatGPT with your question">
    No upload needed, which also means it does not touch your upload allowance at all.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Callout variant="tip" title="Keep the timestamps">
They cost you nothing and they earn their place. Ask for timestamps in the answer and you can jump
straight to the two minutes you actually needed, instead of scrubbing through forty.
</Callout>

## Asking for something better than a summary

A summary of a video is usually as unsatisfying as a summary of a meeting. More useful requests:

**For a tutorial:**

> From this transcript, give me the steps in order, as a numbered list, with the timestamp each one
> starts at. Leave out the introduction and the sponsorship section.

**For a talk or interview:**

> What is the main claim, what evidence is given for it, and what is asserted without support? Quote
> the line each point came from.

**For deciding whether to watch it at all:**

> In five bullets, what does this video actually cover? Then tell me whether it addresses [your
> specific question], and at what timestamp.

That last one is the highest value use of this by a distance. Three minutes to find out a forty
minute video does not answer your question is a good trade.

## What a transcript misses

Speech only. Anything demonstrated on screen and not narrated is absent.

That matters most for tutorials, where "and then you click here" is the whole instruction and the
transcript records it as exactly that. For a talking-head video, an interview or a lecture, a
transcript loses very little.

If the visuals carry the content, screenshot the important moments and upload them as images
alongside the transcript. Images are capped at <Fact k="maxImageSize" /> each with a ceiling of about
100 per conversation.

## Long videos

Auto-generated transcripts are wordy, since speech is repetitive. A ninety minute video can produce a
transcript long enough to matter.

If it approaches <Fact k="maxTextTokens" />, it gets truncated silently. Check by asking what the
final thing discussed was. If it cannot say, it did not reach the end, which for a video with a
conclusion is the part you wanted.

## Several videos at once

The same two-pass approach that works everywhere else. Summarise each transcript separately into the
same structure, then compare:

> Here are structured notes from six videos on the same topic. Where do they agree, where do they
> conflict, and which one actually explains the mechanism rather than describing the outcome?

Genuinely useful for working through a playlist, and it keeps attribution intact, which uploading six
transcripts at once does not.
