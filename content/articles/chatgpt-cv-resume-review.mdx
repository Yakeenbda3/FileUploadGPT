Do not ask it to review your CV. Ask it to argue for rejecting you, or to act as a hiring manager
with sixty applications and ten minutes. A review of something you obviously wrote gets you
encouragement. Adversarial framing gets you the things a real reader would notice and not tell you.

<Callout variant="warn" title="Before you upload it">
A CV holds your name, contact details, employer names and dates. On consumer plans, content may be
used to improve model performance unless you have turned that off in ChatGPT's data controls.

Either check that setting first, or replace your name, address, phone number and current employer
with placeholders. The feedback is exactly as good either way, since nothing useful depends on your
actual phone number.
</Callout>

## The formatting test, which people skip

Do this before any content feedback, because it tests something the content feedback cannot.

Upload the exact file you send to employers and ask:

> Read back the contents of this CV exactly as you received it: every heading, every job title, every
> date, in order.

If what comes back is garbled, sections are missing, or dates have detached from their jobs, that is
a finding. Applicant tracking systems parse CVs the same broad way, by extracting text, and a CV that
does not extract cleanly can be mangled before a human sees it.

The usual culprits are text inside columns, text boxes, tables used for layout, headers and footers,
and text embedded in images. That last one is the worst: a name in a graphic is invisible to
extraction entirely.

## Prompts that produce real criticism

**The rejection argument:**

> You are a hiring manager for [role]. Argue the case for rejecting this candidate at the CV screen.
> Be specific and cite the CV.

**The ten minute screen:**

> You have sixty applications and ten minutes. What do you notice about this one in the first fifteen
> seconds, and would it make your shortlist? Why not?

**The evidence check:**

> Which claims here are stated without evidence? For each, what would evidence look like?

This one is consistently the most useful. "Led a team" and "led a team of six through a migration
that cut deploy time by half" are the same claim with and without evidence, and CVs are full of the
first kind.

**The gap check:**

> What questions would an interviewer ask about this CV that the candidate would rather not be asked?

Unemployment gaps, short stints, unexplained moves. Better to find them now.

## Tailoring to a job

The strongest use of this, and it is a comparison rather than a review.

> Here is a job advert and my CV. Produce a table: requirement, evidence in my CV, strength of that
> evidence, and where it appears. Mark any requirement I have not evidenced at all.

The unevidenced rows are the output. They are either things to add, because you have done them and
did not say so, or things to be honest with yourself about.

Then:

> Rewrite my three most relevant bullets to speak directly to the top three requirements, without
> inventing anything I have not claimed.

That last clause matters. Without it you get impressive bullets describing someone else.

## What it cannot tell you

**Whether you will get the job.** It has no idea about the other applicants, the internal candidate,
or the budget.

**What is normal in your industry or country.** CV conventions differ enormously. Length, photographs,
personal details, what goes at the top. It will apply a general convention confidently.

**Whether your experience is genuinely strong.** It can tell you whether your CV communicates your
experience. Those are different questions and only one of them is a document problem.

## Keep the file clean

Since parsing is part of what is being tested, a plain, single-column layout in a standard font
survives every system it meets. Elaborate design is worth much less than it looks, and it is what
breaks extraction.

If ChatGPT reads your CV back perfectly, most automated systems will too, and that is worth more than
any single piece of wording advice on this page.
