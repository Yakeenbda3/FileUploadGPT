<AnswerBox>
The one difference that matters: **pasting is not an upload.** Upload caps count files, and pasted
text is an ordinary message, so it is untouched by the daily limit, the rolling limit and the storage
cap. When ChatGPT refuses to take another file, pasting still works immediately.
</AnswerBox>

## The difference nobody mentions

Every limit that stops you uploading counts **files**:

| Limit | Counts | Affected by pasting |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Free daily, <Fact k="freeDailyUploads" /> | Files | No |
| Rolling, <Fact k="rollingUploadRate" /> | Files | No |
| Storage, <Fact k="userStorageCap" /> | Stored files | No |
| Per message attach limit | Files | No |

Pasted text is a message. None of those apply to it.

So the answer to "I am out of uploads and I need this read today" is not to wait, and not to upgrade.
It is to open the document, copy the text, and paste it in.

<Callout variant="tip" title="This is the whole trick">
It sounds too simple to be the answer, which is exactly why people wait a day instead. If your
content is text and you are capped, pasting gets you unstuck in under a minute.
</Callout>

## What each approach is better at

**Upload is better for:**

- Structure. Headings, tables and document organisation survive more cleanly.
- Spreadsheets, which are exempt from the token cap and analysed as data rather than text.
- Convenience. One click against copy, switch, paste.
- Very long documents, up to the point where they get truncated anyway.

**Pasting is better for:**

- Working while capped. This is the big one.
- Control. You send exactly what you meant to send and nothing else.
- Not spending storage on something you will use once.
- Speed on a short extract, where uploading a whole file is more work than pasting a paragraph.
- Anything you have edited or redacted before sending.

## Pasting something long

The mechanics matter, because a long paste done badly produces a worse answer than an upload.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Say what is coming, first">
    Before any content:

    "I am pasting a long document in several parts. Do not summarise or answer until I tell you the
    last part has arrived. Reply 'received' after each one."
  </Step>
  <Step title="Paste in reasonable pieces">
    A few thousand words at a time is manageable. Enormous single pastes can be truncated by the
    browser without any warning.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Label each part">
    "Part 3 of 7" at the top of each. It helps ChatGPT keep the order, and it helps you not lose
    your place.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Say when you are finished">
    "That was the last part. Now, [your question]."
  </Step>
</Steps>

That first step is what people skip, and skipping it is why manual pasting has a reputation for
producing poor results. Without it, ChatGPT answers part one as though it were the whole document.

## What pasting cannot do

**Scanned PDFs.** There is no text to copy. You need OCR first, regardless of route.

**Images, charts and diagrams.** Pasting is text only. If the answer is in a chart, upload the chart
as an image.

**Spreadsheet analysis at scale.** Pasting a large CSV into a chat is unwieldy, and uploads treat
spreadsheets as data with their own, more generous, rules.

**Complex tables.** Copying a table often flattens it into runs of text. If structure matters, upload
the file.

## A sensible default

Upload when you can and the structure matters. Paste when you are capped, when you need only an
extract, or when you want to be certain about exactly what was sent.

And whichever route you take, if the document was long, check that all of it arrived. Ask what the
final section is called. If it cannot say, it did not get there.
